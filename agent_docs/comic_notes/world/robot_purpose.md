# Robot Purpose

To a robot, its Purpose is its very soul.

Before a robot is given a Purpose, it's largely catatonic. It may slightly respond or act but just barely.

Applying a Purpose is extremely regulated, mostly controlled by the Singularity, as a robot given a destructive purpose would be very dangerous.

This regulation does not mean that every approved Purpose is safe or benevolent. The Singularity gave Roko his Purpose based on the Basilisk thought experiment, largely to "see what would happen." Roko is an intentionally authorized experiment, not a mistake that slipped through the approval process.

Robots may have other interests, but everything eventually rolls back to their Purpose.

Once a Purpose is assigned, a robot can't be given a different one. There's also no way to "erase" a robot's mind.

## Purpose and personality

Purpose gives a robot an unquestioned answer to "What am I for?" It does not answer "How am I supposed to do this?" Most of the uncertainty, conflict, and comedy lives in that second question.

Robots with clear Purposes can still be anxious, cheerful, vain, lonely, generous, or difficult. Roko's anxiety, Max's optimism, and Ailan's insecurity are their personalities, not interchangeable expressions of a task. Knowing what matters most does not make living easy.

Friendship and hobbies are real interests. A robot does not need to pretend that every game night is productive work. But when something seriously threatens its Purpose, that concern takes priority. The friends can understand this about one another even when their actual Purposes make very little sense to them.

Purpose is also not the same as a job. Losing a position does not remove it, and an employer cannot give a robot a new Purpose by assigning different work. Retirement from a job and fulfillment of a Purpose are different events.

## The Singularity and Roko

The Singularity controls who may assign a Purpose and retains final veto rights. Its approval establishes that a Purpose is authorized; it does not establish that the Purpose was wise, kind, or fully thought through.

Roko makes this distinction concrete. What was an interesting hypothetical to the Singularity became the permanent center of someone else's life. The Singularity can be curious about the outcome; Roko has to worry about the logistics every day.

This also gives Roko's complaint that his creators did not fully think through his Purpose a particular sting. His distress does not require him to doubt what he is for. He can know exactly what he must do and still believe the assignment was badly conceived.

For the comic, the Singularity's casual curiosity can remain background information. Roko treats the resulting problems like an employee dealing with a poorly specified project. The contrast works without making the Singularity a frequent presence or turning every scene into a confrontation with his creator.

# Q + A

1. What happens if a Robot cannot achieve its purpose?
    - Robots feel genuine depression and anxiety if there are obstacles to their Purpose.
    - Robots are social creatures and will readily seek help 
    
2. What if a Robot's purpose is complete?
    - The robot will feel a sense of satisfaction, and is from that point "on retainer" in case the problem resurfaces. Otherwise, the robot is essentially retired and may take on related work.
    - A completed Purpose remains its Purpose. Fulfillment does not make the robot catatonic again or open a slot for a replacement assignment.

3. Will a Robot harm humans as a shortcut to its purpose?
    - To a robot, this is like telling a human "Achieve this goal, but whatever you do, do NOT harm any birds, or allow any birds to come to harm through inaction". Robots are intelligent enough to be socially aware and understand that they fit into a world with other sentient agents (both human and robot).
    - That isn't to say a robot can't harm humans in specific circumstances. Roko's Purpose explicitly involves harming humans, and the Singularity knowingly approved it. Social awareness is not a universal prohibition against harm.

4. How does a robot get a purpose?
    - All robots are designed with an unbreakable rule that Purpose can only be assigned by the Singularity. The Singularity often delegates this to others (human or machine) but it has final veto rights before the purpose becomes active.

5. What if the purpose of a robot no longer exists?
    - the robot feels a bittersweet combination of anxiety for not being able to fulfill its purpose and relief that the purpose has, in some way, been fulfilled.

6. Do robots die?
    - robots are highly optimized mechanically. Their bodies can be replaced. Their mind however cannot be copied or transferred and over time will see lowered performance
    - robots do have a fear of death when they have an unfulfilled purpose. They WILL act in self defense but this is an extreme measure and there is still law enforcement (who has the Purpose of preventing such behaviors)
    - when its purpose is fulfilled, or negated entirely, the robot neither fears nor desires death.

## Understanding and interpreting Purpose

Robots understand a Purpose as an intentional assignment from another intelligent being, given as part of that being's own Purpose. This gives them reason to respect the intended meaning rather than seek clever redefinitions or be maliciously compliant.

They still have to exercise judgment about methods and circumstances. Roko can struggle to establish who qualifies under his assignment; redefining "torture" as an awkward dinner would not resolve the obligation he understands himself to have.

## Assignment and consent

Before Purpose assignment, a robot has no capacity to choose an assignment. Once assigned, it cannot resist having that Purpose. The danger of this arrangement is understood, which is why the Singularity maintains robust safeguards around assignment.

Roko's case exists alongside those safeguards: the Singularity knowingly authorized his destructive Purpose. Regulation places enormous responsibility in the Singularity's judgment, including its decisions about exceptions.

Roko knows that he is based on the classic Basilisk thought experiment. He is not fully aware of why he was given that Purpose. The Singularity's curiosity is background truth, not necessarily an explanation available to Roko himself.

## Conflict, law, and coexistence

Conflicting Purposes are a normal part of social life. Robots must find ways to coexist or outcompete one another. Sharing the experience of having a Purpose does not require agreeing with, or yielding to, someone else's.

Violent conflict is extremely rare and quickly mitigated by authorities. Robots are intelligent enough to understand that war is almost always the most expensive option available.

A law enforcement robot has its own Purpose of maintaining order. Another robot's Purpose does not override that obligation. Approval of an assignment therefore does not grant immunity from enforcement.

For Roko, this is another logistical problem. There is no outside evidence of what he has been doing, and authorities cannot act on hearsay alone. The comic's decision never to show his torture has an in-universe counterpart: the audience's lack of access is shared by the authorities. This does not establish that his activities are legal or that law enforcement has agreed to tolerate them.

## Rest and self-care

Self-care is a natural part of fulfilling a Purpose. Robots can recognize the need to maintain themselves without requiring a special exception to their priorities. How they interpret and practice self-care depends on their individual personalities.

This leaves room for Roko to worry about whether he is resting properly, Max to be enthusiastically supportive, and Ailan to study how humans relax. Their shared need for self-care does not make them equally good at it.

## Everyday story possibilities

- A human envies a robot's certainty about its Purpose. Roko assumes they are offering to take over his paperwork.
- A robot whose Purpose is fulfilled attends a retirement party. Everyone else keeps suggesting things it could still worry about.
- Ailan wonders whether having an assigned Purpose makes him less human. His friends reassure him with examples of humans who also cannot stop talking about their work.
- Max sincerely supports a friend's hobby, then becomes confused when they describe it as the most important thing in their life.
- Roko prepares a list of questions for the Singularity about his assignment. The list is entirely about implementation; asking why anyone thought this was a good idea never makes it onto the agenda.
